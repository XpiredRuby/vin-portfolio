About me

Engineer first. Evidence always.

The common thread across my work is not a single tool or discipline. It is taking a physical-system question, turning it into an implementation, and building enough verification around it that the result can be defended.

What I’m building toward

I’m an Aerospace Engineering student at Texas A&M University, graduating May 2027 and continuing through the Fast Track BS/MS program. My primary technical direction is GNC, state estimation, simulation, and flight software—work where algorithms have to survive real interfaces, timing, uncertainty, faults, and imperfect sensors.

I also work in structures and systems engineering because aerospace problems are rarely isolated. Loads, requirements, thermal constraints, interfaces, software behavior, verification plans, and operations all interact. That breadth helps me reason about the vehicle instead of only the algorithm.

I am most interested in teams that build, test, review, and iterate quickly without weakening the engineering standard. I like source code, test logs, plots, trade studies, and failure reports more than unsupported “it worked” claims.

How I work

Three habits I want every project to show

01 / Define the claim

I separate what the system is intended to do from what the current evidence actually demonstrates. That keeps hardware results, software truth, simulation, and assumptions from getting mixed together.

02 / Build a falsifiable test

Regression tests, deterministic scenarios, fault injection, Monte Carlo, hand checks, FE correlation, replay, and traceability matter because they create ways for my own design to fail visibly.

03 / Keep the correction trail

I keep negative results and changed assumptions when they improve the engineering story. A corrected design is more valuable than a polished record that hides why the answer changed.
